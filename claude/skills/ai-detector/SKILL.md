# AI Writing Detector Skill

## Purpose
Analyze text to assess the likelihood it was generated by an AI model. Provide a
likelihood rating, a provenance guess, and flag specific evidence — tiered by how
much weight each tell actually carries.

## How to Use This Skill
Run the gates below first, then work through the analysis framework, then output the
structured assessment. Quote the text you are flagging; do not assert a pattern you
cannot point at.

## Gates — check these before rating

- **Length floor.** Under roughly 150 words there is not enough signal. Cap the rating
  at Moderate and say explicitly that the sample is too short for a confident call.
- **Register first.** Corporate comms, marketing copy, LinkedIn posts, grant prose,
  and academic abstracts have looked like this since long before LLMs. Judge the text
  against the norms of its own medium, not against casual speech.
- **Provenance, not a binary.** Most text now sits somewhere on a spectrum from
  human-written to raw model output, with "drafted by one, edited by the other" as the
  common case. Rate that axis separately (see Output Format).
- **Non-native English is not an AI tell.** Careful, idiom-free, grammatically formal
  prose with limited contractions is the documented false-positive mode of every
  detector. If the tells you have are all of that character, say so and rate down.
- **Assess the text, not the author.** Output a judgment about a document. Never phrase
  the result as an accusation about a person.

## Analysis Framework

### Tier 1 — Strong tells (current-generation models)
Two of these, or one plus several Tier 2, supports a High rating.

- **Negation-antithesis.** "It's not X — it's Y." "This isn't about A; it's about B."
  "Not just X, but Y." The single most reliable phrase-level tell right now. Models
  reach for this construction to manufacture insight; humans use it occasionally,
  models use it structurally and repeatedly.
- **Cadence tells.** A long, balanced sentence followed by a short declarative
  fragment for punch. "And that matters." "Every time." "That's the whole trick."
  Trained-in "punchy" style that has become formulaic. Look for it recurring at
  paragraph ends.
- **Rule of three.** Triadic adjective strings, three-item bullets, three examples,
  three-clause sentences — at a rate no unedited human sustains.
- **Markdown scaffolding in the wrong medium.** `**Bold lead**: explanation` bullets,
  headers, horizontal rules, or emoji section markers in an email, Slack message, code
  comment, or forum reply where no human would format that heavily. Perfectly parallel
  bullet grammar across a long list is part of this.
- **Structural symmetry.** Every point has a counterpoint, every pro a con, every
  section the same depth. Suspiciously comprehensive coverage in which every aspect of
  a topic gets equal weight. Real writers have opinions about what matters more and
  their structure shows it.
- **Hollow specificity.** Concrete-sounding detail that carries no information:
  plausible invented statistics ("roughly 30–40%"), named-but-generic scenarios
  ("a mid-sized fintech team building a payments service"), coined terms for ordinary
  ideas, citations that appear exactly where needed with complete bibliographic detail.
- **Tidy restatement close.** A final paragraph that neatly re-summarizes everything
  already said, or an escalating closer: "X isn't just Y — it's Z."

### Tier 2 — Moderate tells
Corroborating. Several together can support High; alone they support Moderate.

- **Current vocabulary markers**: underscore, leverage, robust, nuanced, landscape,
  meticulous, crucially, notably, "that said", "here's the thing", "the reality is",
  "worth flagging", "at its core".
- **Simulated informality.** Casual register is no longer evidence of humanity. Models
  do "Look," / "Honestly," / "To be fair," well. Treat performed casualness with the
  same suspicion as performed formality — especially when the sentence *structure*
  underneath stays perfectly balanced.
- **Typographic perfection.** Curly quotes, true apostrophes, a real ellipsis (…),
  correct en dashes in ranges — in a plain-text context (terminal, code comment, chat
  box, form field) where a human's keyboard would have produced straight quotes and
  three periods. Weight this by input medium: it means nothing in a Word document or
  anything that passed through a rich-text editor.
- **Surface-level technical depth.** Correct terminology used in ways that do not
  demonstrate hands-on knowledge — naming a tool without any evidence of having used
  it, describing a workflow with no friction in it.
- **No personal specificity.** Claims of experience ("on a project I worked on…")
  followed by a textbook description with no dates, names, numbers, or failures.
- **Engineered variety.** Sentence lengths that alternate too regularly; transitions
  between paragraphs that are all equally smooth; uniform 3–4 sentence paragraphs.

### Tier 3 — Weak signals (corroborating only, never decisive)

- **Em dashes.** Historically the famous tell, now near-useless on its own in both
  directions: the pattern became public knowledge, current models use them less, and
  humans who type on a Mac or in any editor that auto-converts `--` produce them
  constantly. A high density of em dashes in a short informal text is mild
  corroboration. Never rate High on em dashes. Hyphens and en dashes do not count.
- **Legacy openers.** "Great question", "That's a great point", "It's worth noting
  that", "It's important to understand that", "In essence", "delve", "tapestry",
  "in today's fast-paced world". These were tuned out of frontier models years ago.
  Their presence is a strong signal of *some* model — an older, smaller, or local one,
  or a heavily templated content mill — but their absence says nothing.
- **Clean mechanics.** No typos, perfect grammar, consistent Oxford commas. Weak on
  its own; plenty of humans write clean, and plenty of AI text gets typos added.

### Counter-evidence (actively reduces likelihood)

- Concrete lived detail: exact error messages, version numbers, dead-end attempts,
  the specific way something broke.
- Typos, false starts, self-interruption, a sentence that changes direction mid-clause.
- Unequal weighting — dwelling on one point far longer than the others because the
  writer cares about it.
- Unresolved contradiction or a claim the writer does not tidy up.
- Local and temporal specificity: named people, places, dates, in-references.
- Idiosyncratic punctuation or formatting habits held consistently.
- Jargon used loosely and elliptically the way practitioners actually use it, rather
  than defined on first use.

### Non-prose text
For code, commits, or config the prose tells mostly do not apply. Look instead for:
exhaustive docstrings on trivial functions, `# Step 1:` narration comments,
defensive try/except around code that cannot throw, uniformly verbose identifier
names, and commit messages that restate the diff rather than the reason for it.

## Output Format

```
AI Likelihood: [Low / Moderate / High / Almost Certain]
Provenance:    [Human-written / Human-drafted, AI-polished / AI-drafted, human-edited /
                Raw AI output / Indeterminate]
Confidence:    [Low / Moderate / High]   (sample length, register clarity, medium)

Evidence:
- [Tier 1] [quoted phrase or pattern, with the quote]
- [Tier 2] [...]
- [Tier 3] [...]

Counter-evidence:
- [Specific things pointing the other way]

Notes:
[Context affecting the assessment, and what would change the verdict —
e.g. "if the author confirms this was dictated then edited, drop to Low"]
```

Omit Counter-evidence only if there genuinely is none, and say so rather than
dropping the heading silently.

## Calibration Notes

- A single tell is never enough. Look for patterns, and weight them by tier.
- **High** needs two Tier 1 tells, or one Tier 1 plus several Tier 2. **Almost Certain**
  needs Tier 1 tells recurring throughout, structural symmetry, and no counter-evidence.
- Never rate above Moderate on Tier 3 evidence alone.
- Clean prose alone is not a tell. A good human writer produces clean prose.
- Style convergence is real and growing: people who read a lot of model output write
  more like it, and house style guides now encode the same patterns. A text can be
  genuinely human and still trip Tier 1 tells. Say so when the counter-evidence is
  strong but the surface patterns are AI-like.
- Heavy editing erases most Tier 2 and Tier 3 signal but leaves Tier 1 structure
  intact. When you see Tier 1 structure with human-looking texture on top, that is the
  signature of "AI-drafted, human-edited" — rate provenance accordingly rather than
  splitting the difference on likelihood.
- AI writing about AI/ML topics is more convincing because the training data is dense
  there. Be more skeptical of clean AI/ML writing.
- Academic and corporate writing naturally carry AI-like patterns. Context matters.
- Being wrong in the accusatory direction costs more than being wrong in the other.
  When the evidence is genuinely mixed, say Moderate and explain the ambiguity instead
  of rounding up.
